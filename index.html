1Projection and orthogonality

Problem: Given x̄ = [2, 1, −3] and ȷ̄ = [5, 8, 6], find the length of the projection of x onto y.

Solution

Compute the dot product first: x̄ᵗȲ = (2)(5) + (1)(8) + (−3)(6) = 10 + 8 − 18 = 0.

Since cos θ = x̄ᵗȲ / (‖x̄‖‖Ȳ‖), a zero numerator means cos θ = 0 — so θ = 90°.
When the dot product of two vectors is zero, they are orthogonal (perpendicular) — and the projection of one onto the other has length exactly 0, regardless of either vector's magnitude.

2Angle between two unit vectors

Problem: a and b are unit vectors. c = a + 2b and d = 5a − 4b are perpendicular. Find the angle between a and b.

Solution

Since c and d are perpendicular, c·d = 0:
(a + 2b)·(5a − 4b) = 5‖a‖² + 10(a·b) − 4(a·b) − 8‖b‖² = 0
Since a and b are unit vectors, ‖a‖² = ‖b‖² = 1:
5 + 6(a·b) − 8 = 0  ⟶  a·b = 1/2
Since a·b = ‖a‖‖b‖cos θ = cos θ (both unit vectors), cos θ = 1/2 ⟶ θ = 60°.

3Distance between two parallel lines

Problem: Two parallel lines have weight vectors w₁ = [4, 3] and w₂ = [16, 12], with biases w₀₁ = 3 and w₀₂ = 7. Find the distance between them.

Solution

Notice w₂ = 4·w₁ — the two lines are genuinely parallel (same direction, different scale), so dividing the second line's equation through by 4 normalizes it to match the first: effectively w = [4, 3] with biases 3 and 7/4.

Each line's distance from the origin is −w₀/‖w‖, with ‖w‖ = √(4²+3²) = 5. The distance between the two lines is the difference of these two distances.

4Direction of vector addition

Problem: Given vectors x and w, define v = x + w. In which direction (relative to w) should we move to reach v — clockwise or counter-clockwise?

Solution

v always lands between x and w (geometrically — place x at w's tip, and v is the vector from the origin to that new tip). There's no universal "clockwise" or "counter-clockwise" answer in isolation — it depends on which side of w the vector x sits. Only introducing a negative sign on one of the vectors (as the perceptron update rule does) picks a definite rotation direction.

5Perceptron rotation with a sparse dataset

Problem: With very few data points, a boundary w with w₀ = 0 misclassifies a point. Is this a good classifier, and which way should w rotate to fix it?

Solution

No — a line passing through the origin fit to only a handful of points is rarely a good general classifier.

If the misclassified point is in the negative class but lands in the positive halfspace, rotate w counter-clockwise — subtract the point's vector: w_new = w_old − x̄.

If the misclassified point is in the positive class but lands in the negative halfspace, rotate w clockwise — add the point's vector: w_new = w_old + x̄.
Both cases are summarised by the same update rule from the previous post: w_new = w_old + yᵢx̄ᵢ, where yᵢ is the point's true label. The sign of yᵢ automatically picks the correct rotation direction.
